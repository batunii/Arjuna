1
The idea · in things we already do

We already remove distraction — for space and for sound. Just not for sight.

Space

The library carrel

When I really needed to focus, I sat in a library carrel: wooden walls on three sides. A physical barrier that simply removes everything that isn't the book.

Sound

Noise-cancelling headphones

ANC doesn't make the lecture louder. It makes everything else quieter. We accept — and pay for — subtraction as the way to hear better.

Sight

Arjuna and the sparrow's eye

In the Mahābhārata, Drona asks his students what they see before they shoot. Only Arjuna answers: "only the eye of the bird." The oldest description of perfect visual focus — everything else gone.

This project builds the third column: visual noise cancellation, in software, on a headset anyone can buy.

Framing follows the "visual noise cancellation" reading of Diminished Reality (ACM 2024); Mori, Ikeda & Saito 2017 survey diminishment as a first-class operation.
2
The problem · distraction has a measurable price

Peripheral distractors more than double your errors

1.33 → 3.15 commission errors

In a 2025 virtual-classroom study (N = 66), adding peripheral distractors to a sustained-attention task raised error counts ~2.4×, p < .001. Attention capture is involuntary: you don't choose to look — motion and luminance in the periphery pull the eye before you can veto it.

  • Modern environments are engineered against focus: open-plan offices, screens beside workspaces, advertising built to capture the gaze.
  • Every capture carries a switching cost on the task you actually wanted to do.
  • Willpower doesn't scale. Barriers do — that's why the carrel and the headphones exist.

The question: can a headset play the role of the carrel walls — for vision?

Backed by: Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 2025 (N=66, Go/No-go, p<.001) — the effect H1 is built on. Itti, Koch & Niebur 1998 (involuntary capture by peripheral motion/luminance).
3
The gap · two ways to guide the eye

The field adds arrows. This work subtracts the pull.

Additive guidance (well studied)

Draw attention TO the target

Arrows, halos, attention funnels (Biocca 2006). Works, but adds clutter of its own, and does nothing about the thing that's distracting you — it just shouts louder.

Subtractive guidance (this work)

Remove attention FROM everything else

Dim, mute or re-grade the periphery so it stops pulling. This is Diminished Reality (DR) — like ANC, it cancels the noise instead of amplifying the signal.

  • Users want diminishment: Cheng et al. (CHI 2022) found people ask for clutter to be hidden when focusing.
  • DR can cut workload but risks situational-awareness loss (McLaughlin 2025; Murphy 2021) — so the evaluation must measure both sides. That trade-off becomes hypothesis H2b.
  • Both closest prior studies ran in VR simulations. Nobody had run subtractive guidance on real passthrough, on consumer hardware. That's the delta.
Backed by: Biocca et al. 2006 (attention funnel); Cheng et al. CHI 2022; McLaughlin et al., Human Factors 2025; Murphy et al. 2021; Mori et al. 2017 (DR survey).

The instrument · why this is possible now

A video-see-through headset makes every visible pixel software

Optical see-through

Can't subtract

HoloLens-style optics only ADD light to the real world. You cannot darken a lamp you see through glass. Subtractive guidance is physically impossible there.

Video see-through

Can, in principle

Quest 3 shows you the world as video on an opaque screen. Every pixel you see was rendered — so in principle, every pixel can be modified.

Consumer scale

Deployable

This isn't a lab rig. Quest 3 costs ~€500 and is in millions of homes. If subtraction works here, it works on hardware people already own.

In principle, the perfect instrument. In practice, there's a wall →

Context: no current VST headset reaches normal visual acuity through passthrough, and Quest 3 degrades further in low light (arXiv 2601.02805, 2026) — this shapes the architecture on the next slides.
5
The wall · the compositing constraint

The OS owns the passthrough. An app cannot touch it.

  • On Quest 3, passthrough is composited by the operating system. An app can't read it, can't shade it, can't spatially mask it.
  • All an app may do: draw on top (with transparency), or apply a global colour transform to the whole feed.
  • The 2025 Passthrough Camera Access (PCA) API finally grants the raw camera image — but it is one mono camera, 1280×960, ~85–90° field of view, versus the native stereo ~110° view the user actually sees.

So: full pixel control exists only where image quality is worst.

"Dim everything except what matters, without degrading what matters" — and you're not allowed to edit the image.The problem, stated as a compositing-rights problem
Backed by: Meta Passthrough & PCA documentation; on-device PCA processing throttles within 5–10 min at 720p30 (arXiv 2509.18929) — the thermal ceiling for camera-fed modes.

Building it · the first working system (June 2026)

Version 1: a filter overlay on real passthrough — and it worked, live

  • A translucent sphere composited over the real OS passthrough. Inside the chosen window: alpha = 0 — untouched, full-quality real world. Outside: the filter.
  • For the blur filter, the periphery's pixels were synthesized from the PCA camera feed — so an overlap: native passthrough where you look, filterable camera video where you don't.
  • Three modes, walking around a real room: Dynamic (gaze-following window, grey-dimmed periphery), Perceptual (eccentricity-adaptive blur + desaturation — two power-law curves, contrast boost at the boundary), and Static (controller-drawn rectangle, hard tunnel).
  • Comfort ideas started here too: light/deep presets, effects easing off while the head scans and returning when it settles.

It proved the hybrid was possible on real passthrough — but its camera sampling was screen-space, and its filter vocabulary topped out at dim + blur. Both limits broke next →

The hardest bug · one camera, two eyes

Mono camera + stereo display = double vision

  • The PCA feed comes from one physical camera. The display shows two eyes.
  • First attempt — sample the camera image in screen space — gave each eye a different pixel for the same world point: frank double vision, immediately sickening.
  • The fix — fuse by world direction, not screen position: every fragment computes its direction from the head, then projects that direction through the camera's real pose + intrinsics (a pinhole model, evaluated per-pixel in the shader). Both eyes ask "what does the camera see in this world direction" — and get the same answer.
// pinhole projection, per fragment (CameraSphereVignette.shader) dz = max(dot(dir, camFwd), 0.001) ndc = ( dot(dir, camRight), dot(dir, camUp) ) / dz uv = ndc / tanHalfFov * 0.5 + 0.5 // real intrinsics, not nominal FOV

Intrinsics (focal length, sensor resolution) are read live from the device — with a graceful fade where a direction leaves the camera's frustum, so the narrow feed degrades softly instead of smearing at its edge.

Lesson: on a stereo display, eye-independence is a correctness criterion, not a nice-to-have.

Implementation: CameraSphereVignetteManager.cs (pose + intrinsics feed); CamUV(), InFovWeight() in CameraSphereVignette.shader.
9
The insight · the window is an absence

Don't render the focus window. Render nothing there.

  • The naive design re-renders the camera feed everywhere and "keeps the window sharp" — but then the window is mono, low-res, narrow, and laggy. The best part of the view would be the worst image.
  • Inverting it: inside the window the shader outputs alpha = 0. Nothing is drawn. The OS passthrough — full stereo, full ~110°, zero added latency — simply shows through the hole.
  • The manipulation lives only in the periphery, exactly where the filters need to act.

One line of shader arithmetic solves the FOV problem and the fidelity problem at once:

t = smoothstep(0, softEdge, max(dAz, dEl)) // 0 inside window → 1 in periphery alpha = t × strength × maxAlpha // window = hole, periphery = effect
You cannot degrade what you never drew.The window-as-absence principle

The window is defined in world angles (azimuth/elevation from the head), so it is a stable region of the world — not a spot glued to your face.

This is the one idea that survived every rewrite: it was born in Version 1's overlay (June 13) and carried through the camera-sphere migration two days later.

Implementation: CameraSphereVignette.shader frag(): the focus rect in az/el radians; smoothstep soft edge per mode (2° for Hard Dark, up to 32° for ColorPop).

The novelty claim · two renderings of one reality, fused

The hybrid composite — no prior art found

The user sees one seamless world, assembled from two renderings of the same reality:

  • Layer 1 (native): the OS passthrough — untouchable, but perfect — visible through the window.
  • Layer 2 (ours): a live shader re-render of the PCA camera feed — imperfect, but fully controllable — covering the periphery.
  • The seam between them is the smoothstep soft edge; alignment holds because both layers are indexed by the same world directions.
  • Prior-art search (2026-07-05): 26 scholarly + 4 code-search queries, 136 public forks of the PCA samples checked, 5 years of venue proceedings.
  • Result: overlay-only systems exist; camera-re-render demos exist; the fused composite — native quality through a window in a live filtered camera sphere — was not found, for any purpose.
  • Claimed carefully: "not found" ≠ "provably first", and the claim is scoped to consumer VST passthrough.
Sources: prior-art search log (framing.md); the architecture this enables is on the next slide.

Architecture · deliberately small

One sphere. One shader. One manager.

Geometry

One inverted sphere

A 10 m sphere pinned to the head each frame, rendered from the inside. Every fragment converts its world direction to (azimuth, elevation) — the coordinate system for everything: window, filters, probes, detections.

Rendering

One shader, 11 modes

All filters are branches of a single fragment shader sharing one mask t. In passthrough it composites as a transparent overlay; on the test bench it shades a 360° video sphere — same code, both worlds.

Control

One manager per scene

Feeds camera pose, intrinsics and mode uniforms; handles window painting (hold trigger, sweep, release to lock) and world-anchoring. The study harness drives it through one interface — experiment logic never touches rendering.

  • World-locked by construction: because the window is in world angles, it stays on the desk when you turn your head. For Hard Dark it goes further — the window's corners are raycast against real geometry (environment depth), pinning the window to the physical object even as you walk.
  • The same shader runs the real-passthrough scene (Block A) and the driving-video scene (Block B) — one manipulation, two test environments.
Implementation: CameraSphereVignette.shader; CameraSphereVignetteManager.cs (passthrough); VideoTestSceneManager.cs (video bench); Meta.XR.EnvironmentRaycastManager for world anchoring.

The modes · 10 designed, 3 evaluated

A ladder of subtraction — from a veil to a blackout

Tier 2 · evaluated

Soft Dark

A 75%-opacity dark veil over the periphery. Events remain visible through it. The minimal, unconfounded manipulation — pure luminance attenuation, no camera needed, so it could run for hours.

Tier 2 · evaluated

Hard Dark

Full blackout (alpha 1.0) outside the window, with a near-binary 2° edge. The strongest manipulation — chosen for the confirmatory test (Block A). World-anchored to real geometry.

Tier 3 · evaluated

ColorPop

Doesn't darken — re-grades salience. Signal colours (red / orange / green) stay vivid; everything else drops to dim grey outside the window. Used in the driving block (Block B).

  • 7 further modes were designed and built (Blur, TintedDark, ChromaticCool, SpotLift, ConspicuitySqueeze, GranulatedPeriphery, OutlinedDark) — trimmed to 3 on-device after piloting, so every evaluated mode gets real statistical power. Nothing was deleted: all 11 remain in the shader.
  • Effects form gradually (~3 s smooth ramp) rather than snapping on — abrupt onsets are exactly what captures attention (Yantis & Jonides 1984), and the system must not become its own distractor.
Backed by: IEEE 2021 (area darkening guides attention in 360° video; desaturation nearly ineffective — why dark modes are the workhorse); Yantis & Jonides 1984 (onset capture).
13
ColorPop · salience re-grading, not darkness

Keep the colours that mean something. Mute the rest.

A four-level salience hierarchy, applied per pixel:

  • Inside window + signal colour (red/orange/green): saturation ×1.7, brightness ×1.15, sigmoid contrast boost — pops.
  • Inside window, other colours: untouched.
  • Outside + signal colour: kept recognisable, but not boosted.
  • Outside, everything else: desaturated toward dim grey (~15% periphery luminance).

The contrast boost implements Sutton et al.'s published recipe verbatim (sigmoid, α=10, β=0.5, strength 0.6) — a peer-reviewed knob, not a hand-tuned one.

  • Detector-free by design. Hue + saturation gates decide what's a signal colour — no ML in the loop, so it cannot miss a traffic light the way a detector can, and it costs nothing thermally.
  • Glare compression: headlight bloom is compressed toward a knee, with a "blown-core guard" so clipped lamp centres aren't dimmed to grey.
  • Night-robust: noise and contrast restoration are clamped so hard light/dark boundaries don't marble.
Backed by: Sutton et al., UIST 2022 ("Look over there!" — saliency modulation, the sigmoid recipe); Patney 2016 (contrast restoration); Tariq 2022 (perceptual noise).
14
Safety carve-outs · subtraction must know what to keep

People and signals punch through the filter

  • Detected objects (traffic lights, stop signs, people) carve their own window in the effect: the filter is cleared over the object, a gentle highlight breathes at ~1 Hz, and a soft dark ring around it adds contrast.
  • Traffic lights and signs: always shown. They are never filtered away, anywhere in the view.
  • People are gated by relevance: shown when close (apparent size as a distance proxy) or near the window's edge ("about to enter"). The acceptance cone widens as a person approaches — geometrically, a constant corridor in metres — so the same pedestrian isn't dropped at their nearest point.
  • Detections come from a pre-baked YOLO pass over the study video (9,170 detections, 97% frame coverage) — an oracle, so the study measures the manipulation, not detector luck. Live YOLO exists for free play, debounced against single-frame ghosts.
  • Strength is graded, never binary: highlights taper smoothly with distance from the window, so nothing pops in and out at a hard boundary.
  • This is where DR earns the right to exist: the McLaughlin/Murphy awareness risk is addressed in the design, then measured in the study (H2b).
Backed by: Waldner 2014 (low-frequency pulsing: attention at low annoyance); Veas 2011 (center-surround salience); McLaughlin 2025 / Murphy 2021 (awareness risk → H2b).

Comfort engineering · the system must not become the distraction

Three rules that keep the effect wearable

Rule 1

Yield when the head moves

Above a per-mode head-speed threshold (ColorPop 35°/s · Soft Dark 50°/s · Hard Dark 70°/s) the effect fades out, and fades back once you settle. An inversion of Norouzi 2018: instead of appearing on motion, the effect gets out of the way on motion. (Disabled during study conditions so the dose stays constant.)

Rule 2

Form slowly, never snap

Effects ramp in over ~3 s (smoothstep). Onset transients are the strongest attention magnets in the literature — the one thing a focus tool must never emit.

Rule 3

Lock texture to the world

Noise, grain and film effects are indexed by world direction, not screen position — the pattern sits still in the room instead of swimming on your eyes when you move.

Interaction follows the same philosophy: you paint the window (hold trigger, sweep, release) rather than aiming a cursor, and the window is world-locked — it belongs to the desk, not to your face.

Backed by: Norouzi 2018 (motion-contingent overlays, inverted); Yantis & Jonides 1984 (onset transients); world-locked "rest frame" reasoning for texture stability.

The failure museum · dead ends, kept as findings

Eight walls we hit — documented, not hidden

Rendering

Screen-space sampling → frank double vision. Fix: world-direction fusion (slide 9).

Platform

Surface-projected passthrough deprecated at SDK v83 — mid-project. Fix: window-as-absence on stable primitives.

Android

Shader.Find() returns null on device (Android strips unreferenced shaders). Fix: serialized material reference.

Detection

640×360 bake silently missed small traffic lights. Fix: full-res tiled baking → 97% coverage.

Tooling

Building into a OneDrive folder — sync locks corrupted Android builds. Fix: non-synced build dir.

Capture

Camera-service screenshots return black frames. Fix: framebuffer capture / scrcpy.

Hardware

Controllers asleep at boot — indistinguishable from a broken build. Fix: boot into the experiment scene + a liveness checklist.

Ops

adb-pushed ledger became read-only to the app (owned by shell) → boot crash mid-pilot. Rule: never push files the app must write.

Design decision · why a driving simulation

Driving footage is the stimulus — not the claim

Why simulated driving for Block B

  • Event density a real street never provides: the study clip carries 9,170 baked detections — lights, signs, pedestrians — arriving on a schedule that is identical for every participant.
  • Controlled & repeatable: same events, same timing, same lighting for all 20 participants — the Cheng (CHI 2022) simulation tradition.
  • The right eccentricities: probes sit exactly where the driving literature says awareness is safety-critical (peripheral detection band 11–23°, inside the ~30° useful field of view that shrinks under load).

And the honest boundary

  • Strict claim-scoping: the write-up never claims "driving performance" or "road safety." Driving footage is a rich, motivating stimulus context — nothing more.
  • Block A runs on real passthrough; Block B in simulation. Together they bracket the feasibility spectrum: if they agree in direction, the simulation methodology gains credibility; if they diverge, the divergence is itself a finding.
Backed by: Cheng et al. CHI 2022 (simulation-based DR evaluation); ISO 17488 / Jahn et al. 2005 (PDT band 11–23°); framing.md §2 (claim-scoping rules).

Study design · one session, three blocks

Within-subjects, N = 20, every comparison paired

Sample

N = 20 analysed

24 recruited (4 spares). Within-subjects: every participant sees every condition — each person is their own control. Modal CHI sample is 12 (Caine 2016); power comes from pairing + many trials.

Power

80% for dz ≈ 0.65

On the primary paired difference-of-differences. The source distractor effect is large (>2× at N=66), so this is a realistic target.

Session

~53 minutes

Fixed block order A → B → C. A must be freshest (primary); C absorbs fatigue — an acknowledged limitation, not an accident.

Balance

Latin squares

Block A: 4×4 Williams square. Block B: order alternated by parity. Block C: 3×3 square. All derived automatically from the participant ID — no experimenter arithmetic.

Block A · primary

Workstation focus

Real passthrough + Hard Dark. Does blacking out the periphery reduce the cost of real distractors? → H1, H3

Block B · secondary

Driving probes

Video sphere + ColorPop. Faster central detection without losing the periphery? → H2a, H2b

Block C · exploratory

Mode sampler

All three modes, 75 s each, free viewing + ratings. Soft Dark's only data. Skippable by pre-registered rule.

Backed by: testing-strategy-v2.md §3; Caine 2016 (CHI sample norms); Cheng 2022, McLaughlin 2025 (within-subjects precedent). Instruments: VRSQ pre+post, NASA-TLX ×6, SUS, ESQ.

Block A · the primary test, on real passthrough

A focal task, two fake phones, and a blackout

The setup

  • Task surface directly ahead; two tablets at ±35° azimuth — the near-periphery zone of maximal involuntary capture — playing clickbait-style distractor reels with scheduled salient bursts.
  • 2×2 within: {vignette Off / Hard Dark On} × {distractors absent / present}, ~3.5 min each, Latin-square ordered.
  • Focus window locked by the experimenter (window size is a controlled dose, not a participant choice — supervisor Point 4).

The task: forced-choice 1-back

  • One shape at a time; every trial answer YES/NO: "same as the previous shape?" 105 trials at 2.0 s pace (3:30).
  • Chosen over a simple Go/No-go because reacting to shapes is bottom-up; the 1-back adds working memory + goal maintenance — the thing distraction actually disrupts (supervisor Point 6).

What is measured

  • Error rate per condition; RT; timeouts.
  • Distractor cost = errors(distractors) − errors(quiet), computed separately with vignette Off and On.
  • H1 statistic = cost(Off) − cost(On). Positive = the vignette buys back errors.
  • Head turns toward the tablets (>30° yaw): count + dwell, logged at 60 Hz — an independent behavioural measure of looking away (supervisor Point 5b).
  • NASA-TLX after each condition (→ H3), plus a "did you notice the reels?" open check.
Backed by: Frontiers 2025 (±peripheral distractor paradigm, errors as the sensitive DV); Hart 2006 (raw TLX); supervisor feedback 2026-07-17 Points 4, 5, 6.

Block B · the driving block, in simulation

Can you gain the centre without losing the edges?

The setup

  • Driving footage on the 360° sphere; focus window pre-set to the windscreen region — identical for everyone.
  • Two conditions: raw video vs ColorPop on. Order alternated by participant parity (10/10).
  • The probe task follows the standardised Detection Response Task lineage (ISO 17488): brief peripheral stimuli, press the trigger when you see one, hit window 100–2500 ms.

The probes

  • 24 per condition: 12 central (<10°, inside the window), 12 peripheral (25–40°, outside), ~300 ms onsets, 6–12 s apart.
  • Placed on real objects from the baked detection track, so a probe rides a real pedestrian or light, moving with it.

What is measured

  • Central: median RT + hit rate → H2a (should improve).
  • Peripheral: hit rate → H2b (must stay within 10 pp — the safety margin: "losing more than one peripheral event in ten to the overlay is an unacceptable awareness cost").
  • False alarms; head yaw/pitch at 60 Hz (head pose as validated gaze proxy — Quest 3 has no eye tracker).
  • Analysis is banded by eccentricity (0–10°, 10–20°, 20–30°, …) — a dose-response curve, not one number (supervisor Point 2).
Backed by: ISO 17488:2016; Jahn et al. 2005; van Winsum 2018 (probe conspicuity); Higgins 2022 / Sitzmann 2018 (head pose ≈ gaze); Lakens 2017 (TOST margin).

The hardest design problem · what should a probe look like?

A fair probe must be filtered like the world it sits in

  • The trap: an opaque marker "pops out" through four separate pre-attentive cues (hard silhouette, flat fill, abrupt onset, background-independent contrast). It would be visible through any filter — measuring nothing (supervisor Point 1: ceiling risk).
  • The fix that matters: the probe is composited inside the shader, after the filter — so a probe in a blacked-out periphery is blacked out too, exactly like a real object there (supervisor Point 3, delivered by construction).
  • Onset is ramped, size ~1.6°, contrast calibrated for an informative 60–85% no-filter hit rate — hard enough to miss, easy enough to measure.

Iterated like an experiment

  • Soft billboard: rejected — a tint, not a modulation; fought the dark modes.
  • Refractive "bubble": rejected — borrows scene contrast; Monte-Carlo showed variance could flip the measured sign.
  • Solid black ring: elegant on paper (black survives dimming unchanged) — failed in pilot: camouflaged by clutter, and disqualifyingly more visible with the filter ON (the flat grey periphery removed its camouflage).
  • Final: black/white "rope" ring — achromatic, contains both contrast polarities, the fiducial-marker principle. Near-opaque (alpha 0.88).
Backed by: Bailey et al. 2009 (subtle gaze direction envelope); Wallis, Dorr & Bex 2015 (local contrast limits detection; clutter masks probes); Franconeri & Simons 2003 (gradual onsets don't capture); probe-target-design.md.

Pilot · Day 1 · early signal, honestly labelled

The direction is right — and the flaws were fixable

Block A (focus task)

  • First version was at ceiling (92–100% accuracy) — the supervisor's predicted failure mode, observed. Direction still favoured the filter: filter rounds made zero commission errors.
  • Hardened v2 (105 trials, 2.0 s pace): ceiling broken (84.6–92.3%). First participant: +7.7 pp accuracy, +16 pp go-accuracy, −133 ms median RT, timeouts halved under the filter — with condition order working against it. (n = 1 — a direction, not a result.)

Block B (driving probes)

  • Clearest gains exactly where the mechanism predicts — the near periphery: 10–20°: 44% → 60%; 20–30°: 33% → 67% hit rate. Other bands within ~10 pp; RT equal or faster under the filter.

And the honest part

  • A confound was caught: in the clean group, every filter run used target set A and every baseline set B — so mid-band "gains" partly reflect set difficulty. Fix: fill the missing cells + model at target level with duration covariates.
  • Duration dominates: targets visible ≤1.5 s were hit 18% of the time regardless of condition — unmeasurable. Pool re-screened at ≥2 s.
  • A probe artifact found: the yellow ring lost contrast on ColorPop's warm boost — a visibility issue, not attention. Fixed by the rope probe.
  • De-facto decoys: targets the system does nothing for were hit at the same rate on and off (50% vs 48%) — no differential suppression of the irrelevant.
Sources: analysis-2026-07-22.md §3–6 (set confound §3; duration §4; decoys §5b; v2 ceiling-break §6c). All numbers are pilot-only.

Near-unanimous perceived focus benefit across participants. The one dissent came from the strongest performer ("no real difference") — who still endorsed the other items.

Boundary not distracting, no eye strain, effect faded from awareness. The comfort engineering (slide 16) appears to be doing its job.

Awareness
9/20

All four participants said they "noticed side events later." Two readings: a cost — or the intended mechanism (peripheral onsets no longer capture attention immediately; deferred, not denied). Behaviour favours the mechanism for task-relevant events.

Key insight: the perceived cost exceeds the measured cost. People feel they lose more periphery than they measurably do — which makes trust in the periphery a design target, not just a statistic.

One more iteration. Then freeze, run N = 20, and report — good or bad

The freeze plan (agreed with supervisor)

  • One final iteration bundle: rope probe, pool re-screened at ≥2 s, hardened Block A task, protocol fixes. Window and dose frozen: 6°×4° + 24° soft edge, periphery ~15% luminance.
  • Then the build is tagged and frozen. Every formal participant runs one configuration — versions are never aggregated.
  • Analysis depth is committed: mixed models at target level, TOST for equivalence, distributions and CIs, paired per-participant plots.

Why every outcome is publishable

  • H1 holds + H2b holds → the system works as claimed.
  • H1 holds, H2b fails → it works, at a quantified awareness cost.
  • H1 refuted → a conclusive, bounded negative ("recovers at most X% of the cost").
  • Manipulation check fails → a finding about the paradigm itself.
