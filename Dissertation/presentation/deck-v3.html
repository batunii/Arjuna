Attention is a bargain. This asks whether it's a good one.

A Diminished-Reality attention-guidance system on a consumer video-passthrough headset — and an eleven-participant test of what it buys and what it costs.

1
The idea · starting from something you already do

We already delete distraction — for space and for sound. Almost never for sight.

Space

The library carrel

Three walls around a desk. Nothing is added to help you read — the room is simply subtracted until only the page is left.

Sound

Noise-cancelling headphones

A everyday consumer product whose entire function is removal. Nobody finds it strange to pay for silence.

Sight

…nothing

For vision we do the opposite: we add arrows, highlights, labels, pop-ups. The visual equivalent of the carrel does not exist as a wearable product.

This project builds the missing one — a wearable that dims what you are not attending to — and then asks the only question that matters: does subtracting the surroundings actually help, and what does it cost you?

The acoustic analogy is the literature's own: Visual Noise Cancellation proposes head-worn AR that "moderates the visual environment" by direct analogy to active noise cancellation.
2
Vocabulary · two words, then everything else follows

Augmented Reality adds. Diminished Reality takes away.

Augmented Reality (AR)

Digital content is added on top of what you see. An arrow floating over a machine part; a name label over a face. Almost all commercial headset software does this.

Its failure mode: the addition becomes one more thing competing for your attention.

Diminished Reality (DR)

Real content is concealed, muted or removed so that what remains is easier to attend to. Formally defined in the literature as "concealing real-world objects and visual clutter to support focus".

Its failure mode is the subject of this dissertation: you may remove something you needed.

Attention guidance
Any technique that changes where a person looks or attends, rather than what information they are given.
Subtractive guidance
Guiding attention by degrading the non-target regions instead of marking the target. The approach taken here.
Cheng, Yin, Yan, Gugenheimer & Lindlbauer, "Towards Understanding Diminished Reality", CHI 2022 — DR definition and the within-subject evaluation pattern this study follows. Mori et al., "A Survey of Diminished Reality", 2017.
3
Why now · the hardware precondition

On a video-passthrough headset, every visible pixel is software.

Video passthrough (video see-through, VST)
The headset is opaque. Outward-facing cameras film the room and the displays show you that video. You are watching a live recording of your surroundings, not looking through glass.
Optical see-through (OST)
Transparent glass with light projected onto it — the older AR approach. Light can only be added: physically incapable of making the real world darker.

This is the precondition for the whole project. Diminished Reality on transparent glasses is close to impossible — you cannot subtract light you never controlled. On a Quest 3 the real world arrives as a texture, and a texture can be dimmed, desaturated, or replaced.

▶
Demo: the filter switching on, live
15–20 s of in-headset capture, filter toggling off→on while the wearer looks around a normal room. This is the "oh, I see" moment — put it early and let it play without narration.
Practical caveat carried through the whole dissertation: Quest 3 passthrough never reaches normal visual acuity and degrades in low light — "The perceptual gap between video see-through displays and natural human vision", arXiv 2026. This is why the tasks here use large, high-contrast stimuli and bright lighting, never fine text.
4
The artefact · one shader, one geometry

A clear window, and a gradient that fades the rest away.

The system keeps a small region of the view completely untouched, then fades progressively toward heavy dimming as you move outward from it. Two numbers define it, and both were chosen from the literature (next slides):

The shipped configuration

Clear core — 6° left/right, 4° up/down from centre. Untouched.
Soft edge — 24° of gradual falloff beyond the core.
Floor — the far periphery settles at ≈15% of its original brightness, never zero.

Degree (°) of visual angle
The unit for "how far from the centre of your gaze". Your thumbnail at arm's length is about 1–2°. A whole hand at arm's length is about 20°. Everything in this deck is measured in degrees from the centre of the view.

Schematic, not a screenshot — the real effect is a per-pixel shader over the camera feed.

The 15% floor is anchored to ITU-R BT.500's reference-surround ratio (0.15 × peak luminance for critical viewing) and sits above the classic ~10:1 task-to-surround comfort limit. A blackout was tried first and rejected: it made the periphery unusable and uncomfortable.

Act I — why subtract? · first, prove there is something to remove

Peripheral distractions more than double your errors. That's the thing being removed.

A subtractive interface is only worth building if the surroundings actually cost you something measurable. They do, and the size of the cost is established independently of this project:

Established finding — not this study's data

In a head-mounted display, adding peripheral video distractors to a sustained-attention task raised commission errors from 1.33 to 3.15 (p < .001, N = 66). Errors — not reaction time — were the sensitive measure.

Commission error
Pressing when you should have withheld. A false alarm. In attention research it is the classic signature of a lapse: the distraction pulled you, and your finger moved before your judgement caught up.

That number is the target. The question this project asks is whether dimming the surroundings gives any of it back.

Act I — why subtract? · why dimming, and not arrows or desaturation

Three separate literatures point at darkening in particular.

1 · Why removal works at all

Cognitive Load Theory

Attention and working memory are finite. Irrelevant visual information consumes capacity that the task needed — "extraneous load". Removing it is therefore not a trick; it is returning capacity.

Buchner et al., on AR and cognitive load.

2 · Why modulating pixels guides

Saliency modulation

Changing the brightness and contrast of real-world regions in AR reliably moves where people look. This is the mechanism the shader implements.

Sutton et al., "Look over there!", UIST 2022 (N = 20, within-subject).

3 · Why dark, not grey

Darkening beats desaturation

In 360° video with eye tracking, area darkening successfully guided attention while desaturation was nearly ineffective. This single result is why the system's workhorse modes are dark ones rather than colour-removal.

"Attention-guiding Methods in Cinematic VR Based on Eye Tracking", IEEE 2021.

And the benchmark to beat: additive guidance already works

The Attention Funnel (an added 3D visual cue leading the eye to a target) cut workload by 18% and improved search by 22%. Additive guidance is not a straw man — it is effective, commercially shipped, and the thing subtractive guidance has to justify itself against. Its known weakness is that it must know where the target is; subtraction does not.

Biocca et al., "Attention Funnel", CHI 2006 — workload −18%, search +22%. The contrast matters for positioning: the funnel needs a target; this system only needs to know where you are already looking.
7
Act I — why subtract? · and the reason to be afraid of it

The same literature that says it helps says it might blind you.

The documented risk

DR lowers cognitive workload but risks loss of situational awareness — you stop noticing things you were not told to ignore. A 2025 Human Factors paper builds an explicit framework for testing whether peripheral manipulation causes dangerous blindness.

Murphy et al. 2021; McLaughlin et al., Human Factors 2025.

Attention tunnelling
When a guidance cue works too well: attention narrows onto the guided region and genuinely important events outside it go unnoticed. The recognised failure mode of all attention guidance, additive or subtractive.
Inattentional blindness
Failing to see something plainly visible because attention was elsewhere. Not an eyesight problem — an attention problem.
The shape of the whole dissertation

A subtractive interface is a bargain: you buy focus, and you pay with awareness of everything else.

Every serious claim in this work is about that exchange rate. Not "does it work" — what does it cost, where, and is the price worth it.

Which gives exactly two things to measure, and therefore exactly two hypotheses.

McLaughlin et al., "Cognitive Aid Design Using Diminished Reality", Human Factors 2025 — within-subject, linear mixed models, focus-vs-awareness trade-off. This is the paper the safety hypothesis is modelled on.

Act I — why subtract? · the two claims, fixed before any data

One buys. One pays. Both were written down before the first participant.

Hypothesis
A specific, falsifiable prediction — stated so that a particular observation would disprove it. "The system is good" is not a hypothesis; "accuracy is higher with the filter on" is.
Null hypothesis
The boring rival explanation, always assumed by default: "there is no difference; what you're seeing is chance." A statistical test never proves a hypothesis true — it only reports how badly the null hypothesis fails to explain the data. Everything on the results slides is an answer to "could this just be noise?"
H1 · what it buys

Dimming the surroundings improves performance on the task you are attending to.

Measured as accuracy on a demanding focus task while realistic distractions play in the periphery.

Refuted if: accuracy is no better, or worse, with the filter on.

H2 · what it costs

It does not cost more peripheral awareness than a safety margin allows.

Measured as the proportion of peripheral events noticed. The pre-set margin was 10 percentage points: losing more than one event in ten to the overlay is an unacceptable price.

Refuted if: peripheral detection drops by more than 10 points.

Note the asymmetry, because it is deliberate. H1 hopes for a difference. H2 hopes for a non-difference — and proving "close enough to the same" needs its own kind of test, with the margin fixed in advance so it cannot be quietly moved afterwards.

The 10-point margin follows the DR focus-versus-awareness framing of McLaughlin 2025 and Murphy 2021; the equivalence-testing procedure for "proving no meaningful difference" is Lakens, "Equivalence Tests", SPPS 2017. Full pre-registered wording in testing-strategy-v2.md §2.

Act II — how do you measure a bargain? · the words on every results slide

Six terms. Once these are clear, every number later reads itself.

Within-subjects / paired
Every person does both conditions, so each is compared only against themselves. This removes the biggest source of noise in a small study — that people simply differ. Every comparison in this deck is paired.
p-value
The probability of seeing a difference this large if the null hypothesis were true and nothing real was happening. Small = "chance explains this badly". The conventional cut-off is 0.05. It is a convention, not a law of nature.
95% confidence interval (CI)
The range of true effects consistent with the data. More useful than a p-value: if the interval excludes zero, "no effect at all" is not among the plausible answers.
Effect size (Cohen's dz)
How big the difference is relative to how much it varies between people. Rough reading: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. A p-value says "probably real"; the effect size says "and worth caring about".
Wilcoxon signed-rank test
A paired test that uses only the ranking of the differences, not their exact sizes. Preferred here because scores pile up against a 100% ceiling, which breaks the assumptions of the ordinary t-test.
Statistical power
The chance of detecting a real effect of a given size with the sample you have. With 11 people this study has roughly 60% power — meaning a real effect would be missed about four times in ten. Low power makes negative results weak, but it does not weaken the positive ones.
Small-sample justification: the modal user-study sample size across all CHI papers is 12 (Caine, CHI 2016); small in-person studies derive power from repeated measures within each person, not from headcount. The two closest DR evaluations ran N = 16 and N = 12.

Act II — how do you measure a bargain? · Test 1 of 2 — what it buys

Task A: hold your attention on one boring thing while the edges try to steal it.

Forced-choice 1-back
A shape appears. You answer one question — is this the same shape as the last one? — and then it happens again, 140 times, for about four and a half minutes. Every trial demands an answer, so there is nowhere to hide and no way to coast.

The distraction. Short-form vertical video — fast cuts, animated captions, the format engineered to be unignorable — plays continuously in the peripheral margins either side of the task. Same clips in both conditions for a given participant.

What is measured. Percentage of trials answered correctly. Plus wrong presses, reaction time, and whether performance decays across the run.

Why the clock is set where it is

Response window 1.7 s, one trial every 2.0 s. The field-standard n-back gives 3.0 s — deliberately slower than this. A pace floor comes from the SART at 1.15 s, but that is a simple detection task, not a judgement. House rule: the window must exceed twice the typical response time, or you are measuring who panics rather than who attends.

▶
Screen capture: Task A running
20–25 s showing the shape stream centre-screen with the video distractors playing in both margins. Record it twice — filter off, then on — and cut them side by side. Nothing communicates the manipulation faster than seeing the margins go dark.
n-back parameters: PsyToolkit reference implementation and Psychometric characteristics of the n-back task, Current Psychology 2025 (500 ms stimulus + 2500 ms blank). Pace floor: Robertson et al. 1997, SART. Against tight deadlines: attentional lapses live in the slow tail of the reaction-time distribution (ex-Gaussian τ), and deadline errors reflect a shifted speed–accuracy criterion rather than reduced ability — a tight window would censor exactly the signal H1 predicts.

Act II — how do you measure a bargain? · Test 2 of 2 — what it costs

Task B: drive, and press whenever you notice something at the edge.

The participant watches recorded driving footage wrapped around them and pulls the trigger whenever they spot a marker appearing somewhere in the scene. 40 markers per run, spread from dead ahead out past 40°.

Detection Response Task (DRT) — the paradigm this copies
An international standard (ISO 17488) for measuring how loaded a driver is: show a small peripheral stimulus at unpredictable intervals, measure whether and how fast it is noticed. A response counts if it lands within 100–2500 ms. Deliberately not tailored to each person, so results compare across studies.
Hit rate
The percentage of markers noticed. This is the number that carries H2: if dimming the periphery blinds people, this is where it shows up.

This is a simulation, and stated as one. Recorded footage gives every participant identical, repeatable, event-dense stimuli — something a real street never provides. The precedent is direct: the closest published DR evaluations were run entirely inside simulations because real DR was not yet feasible.

▶
Screen capture: Task B, both conditions
25–30 s of the driving footage with markers appearing, once filter-off and once filter-on. Ideally freeze on one marker in the periphery so the audience can see it dim as the filter comes on — that is the point of the next slide.
ISO 17488:2016 Detection Response Task; Jahn et al. 2005 peripheral detection as a workload measure. Simulation-based evaluation precedent: Cheng et al., CHI 2022. Response modality (click on naturalistic scene-consistent targets) follows the Road Hazard Stimuli dataset, Behavior Research Methods 2023.

Act II — how do you measure a bargain? · the detail that makes Task B honest

The marker had to be dimmed by the filter too — or the test would cheat.

If the marker were drawn on top of the filter at full brightness, it would stand out more against a darkened background and the filter would look good for a reason that has nothing to do with attention. So the marker is pushed through exactly the same dimming as the scene at its position: full strength inside the clear window, fading like a real object in the periphery.

The consequence, stated plainly

In the filter condition the markers in the periphery were harder to see, not easier. Any improvement in detection there has to be paid for out of attention, not contrast.

Why a striped ring. Four black-and-white arc pairs, 1.6° across. Achromatic, so desaturation does nothing to it. Contrast-complete, so some arc contrasts against any background — the fiducial-marker principle. And dimming multiplies it, which preserves the pattern's internal contrast: it fades honestly without dissolving into flat grey.

Same marker, three positions
Engineered not to pop out: ramped onset because abrupt onset is the strongest involuntary attention grab (Yantis & Jonides 1984) while gradual change is fully visible yet does not capture (Simons et al. 2000; Cole et al. 2011); held static because motion and looming capture (Franconeri & Simons 2003); modulates the underlying pixels rather than overpainting them (Wallis, Dorr & Bex 2015). Target difficulty 60–85% without the filter, because probes that are too easy detect no workload effect at all (van Winsum 2018).

Honesty 1

The versions genuinely differ

The focus task got harder across sessions because the first version was at ceiling. Pooling across versions was permitted by the supervisor on 27 July on the grounds that the variants came out close enough — but it is a real limitation, so pilot and extra are shown separately throughout.

Honesty 2

One run was excluded

One driving run logged 539 wrong presses and its hits were measurably sloppier than everyone else's — some were accidents. That pair is out of Task B. It would have been the second-largest gain, so the exclusion costs the result rather than creating it.

Honesty 3

An analysis error, found and fixed

An earlier version of this analysis joined the wrong target file and used 330 of 880 markers, mis-assigning some angles. Every angle-based number here is recomputed from the correct file. The main finding survived, smaller and cleaner.

Within each participant the version and distraction set are held constant across both conditions, so the paired comparison is unaffected by version drift; only cross-participant pooling is. Ceiling effect = when a task is so easy that scores bunch at 100% and improvement becomes invisible — the reason the task was made harder mid-study.

The order test

In eight of eleven sessions the no-filter driving run came first, so practice always flattered the filter. The final session deliberately reversed it — filter first — so practice worked against it. The filter still won both tasks, and by +27.0 points in the ring. One participant is not a control condition, but it is the first direct check, and it came out the right way.

Not from catching more matches — from withholding more wrong presses. The clearest case fell from 24 wrong presses to 5; the newest from 10 to 6. That gives the effect a nameable mechanism: the filter reduces impulsive responding under visual distraction.

The replication argument

The two phases were collected on different evenings, with different people and a different task version. In three of the four bands the difference changes sign between phases — that is what noise looks like. In the 20–30° ring both phases are positive and both are large. It is the only band where that is true.

Per participant, the ring is significant

+18.6 points, 95% CI +2.5 to +34.7, Wilcoxon p = 0.030, t-test p = 0.028, dz = 0.78, nine of eleven improved. The overall hit rate, by contrast, is +3.9 points at p = 0.44 — and is reported as the null result it is.

Bands are the target's angle from the centre of the view at the midpoint of its life, joined from the authored target pool (880 scored markers). "Filter ≈84%" is the mean strength of the dimming across that band under the shipped 6°×4° + 24° geometry.

The effect needs two things at once — and only one band has both.

Why this is not just "more dimming = more effect"

If the story were simply that dimming helps, the strongest effect would be beyond 30°, where dimming is total. It is not — it is zero there. The effect requires the dimming to be strong and the target to still be within reach of attention. That conjunction is a prediction the geometry made in advance, not a pattern found afterwards.

And inside the ring, it is the hardest case that moves

Markers in the ring that appeared and vanished in under two seconds: 2 of 36 caught without the filter, 9 of 30 with it (5.6% → 30.0%). Markers in the ring lasting longer than two seconds: 65% → 72% — they were being caught anyway.

Brief markers in the neighbouring bands gain only 3–4 points, so this is not a general "the filter helps with brief things" effect. It is specific to brief things in the ring.

"Useful field of view" is the region from which information can be taken in a single fixation without moving the eyes — ≈30° across, and it shrinks under cognitive load. The 20–30° ring therefore sits exactly at the boundary where attention can still be redirected but is not automatic.

Act III — results · the cost, as measured and as felt

The price people report is not the price the task records.

H2 — the measured cost

Not refuted. Not even close to refuted.

The safety margin was a 10-point drop in peripheral detection. Beyond 30° the change is −1.5 points; in the 20–30° ring it is +18.2. Objective peripheral awareness did not fall anywhere.

The feared failure mode — dimming the edges makes people miss things at the edges — did not appear in the data at any angle.

The subjective cost

But people felt it.

Of ten participants: only 4 of 10 denied worrying they would miss something; only 5 of 10 were comfortable not seeing everything clearly; 5 of 10 said side events registered later than usual.

Meanwhile the focus benefit was near-unanimous: 47 of 50 answers pro-filter, and three items answered identically by all ten — easier to hold attention, obvious where to look, and no, they did not look away as often.

Why the gap matters

A system can be safe and still feel unsafe, and felt-unsafe is a real adoption barrier regardless of what the task data says. This is a design finding, not a measurement error: the interface needs to communicate that the periphery is still being watched, not merely watch it.

Evidence it is tunable, not inherent

All four pilot participants said side events registered later. Among the six tested afterwards, only one did. The change tracks the settings freeze between day 1 and day 2 — which makes the perceived awareness cost look like a function of how aggressive the dimming is, rather than a fixed price of the technique.

Questionnaire administered as agree/disagree rather than the designed 7-point scale, so these are counts and remain exploratory. Reverse-keyed items are scored by their disagreement. One item was left blank by every participant and is omitted. The day-1 versus later comparison is an observation across sessions, not a controlled manipulation.

Act III — results · everything that could still be wrong

What would have to be true for this to be wrong.

Alternative 1

People just pressed more

Wrong presses rose with the filter, 7.7 → 12.5 per run (p = 0.22). If the filter simply made people trigger-happy, hit rate would rise without anyone seeing more.

Against it: extra pressing would raise detection everywhere. The gain is confined to one 10°-wide ring and is absent in the three others. Bias does not have a favourite eccentricity.

Alternative 2

The marker got easier to see

A marker drawn on top of a darkened scene would stand out more, producing a detection gain with no attentional content.

Against it: the marker is pushed through the same dimming as the scene. In the filter condition it was dimmer. The confound runs the wrong way.

Alternative 3

Practice, not filter

Eight of eleven sessions ran no-filter first, so the filter run always had more practice behind it.

Against it, partially: the one session that reversed the order reproduced both results. One session is not a control condition — this stays on the list until the order is properly alternated.

Live limitation

Eleven people, ~60% power

A real effect of this size would be missed about four times in ten. The two positive results are not weakened by this; the null results are — "no difference found" here is weak evidence of no difference.

Live limitation

The ring depends on brief markers

Restricting to markers lasting over two seconds cuts the ring effect to +6.9 points. The finding lives in exactly the short-lived markers a pool re-screen was designed to remove — so the next pool must deliberately keep them.

Live limitation

Task versions differ

The focus task changed across sessions. Within each person it is constant, so paired comparisons hold; pooling across people mixes versions. Shown separately as pilot and extra throughout for exactly this reason.

Not on this list because it was found and fixed: an earlier analysis joined the wrong target-pool file and analysed 330 of 880 markers with some angles mis-assigned. Recomputed against the correct file, the ring effect fell from +31 to +18 points and the surrounding pattern became cleaner. The error is recorded rather than quietly corrected.

Act IV — what it means · the best current explanation, and how to kill it

Dimming a region made things in it easier to notice. That needs explaining.

The naive prediction is the opposite of what happened. The marker was dimmed along with the scene, so its absolute visibility fell — and detection went up anyway. Two published findings make that coherent:

1 · Peripheral sensitivity is gated by attention

In head-mounted displays, how well the periphery detects things depends on where attention is allocated, not only on optics. Move attention and peripheral sensitivity moves with it.
Krajancich, Kellnhofer & Wetzstein, ACM TOG 42(4), 2023.

2 · Clutter masks probes

In naturalistic video, local contrast probes are masked by high-edge-density surroundings — busy regions hide them. The filter suppresses that surrounding structure, while the marker's striped pattern was engineered so multiplicative dimming preserves its internal contrast.
Wallis, Dorr & Bex, Journal of Vision 15(8), 2015.

Candidate mechanism: the filter removes the competing clutter faster than it removes the marker. In a quietened periphery, a structured object is the only thing left with internal structure — so it wins a competition it was losing before.

Stated as a candidate, not a result

This study did not test the mechanism. It measured an outcome and found a spatial pattern consistent with one. That distinction belongs in the write-up.

The falsifiable prediction

If clutter suppression is the mechanism, the benefit must scale with how cluttered the scene is at the marker's location — large where the background is busy, near zero against plain sky or road.

That is directly testable with data already on disk: the baked scene detections give an edge-density estimate at every marker. If the effect turns out to be flat across clutter levels, this explanation is wrong and attention reallocation alone survives.

Act IV — what it means · the claim the dissertation actually makes

Subtractive guidance is not a feature you switch on. It's a dose you place.

The wrong question

"Does dimming the periphery help?"

Asked this way the answer is a shrug: overall focus accuracy +3.7 points, overall peripheral detection +3.9 and not significant. A reviewer would reasonably call that thin.

The right question

"Where does dimming the periphery help, and at what strength?"

Asked this way there is a specific, quantified, replicated answer — and a prediction the next study can falsify.

The bargain, priced

On a sustained-attention task under realistic distraction, dimming the surroundings bought 3.7 accuracy points (nine of eleven people, p = 0.024) — and it did so mostly by suppressing impulsive responses rather than by revealing anything new.

The awareness it was supposed to cost never got paid. At the angles the safety margin was written for, detection did not drop. In the 20–30° ring — where the dimming is strong and attention can still reach — detection rose by 18.6 points, and the recovered events were precisely the brief ones nobody was catching before: 2 of 36, up to 9 of 30.

What people felt was a cost, and half of them said so. That gap between the measured price and the felt price is the design problem the next iteration inherits.

Both headline results clear the conventional threshold on both a parametric and a rank-based test, with confidence intervals excluding zero, at n = 11 and ~60% power. Neither is a confirmed finding at this sample size; both are the kind of result that a pre-registered N = 20 either confirms or kills.

Act IV — what it means · six changes, then freeze

Everything needed to turn this into a confirmed result is cheap.

1 · Sample

Reach twenty

The power analysis now puts 80% power at 16–17 participants for both headline effects. The planned twenty clears it with margin. Five more people is the single highest-value action.

2 · Stimulus

Load the hard corner

Put more markers in the 20–30° ring and keep them brief. That cell currently carries about 8 markers per person per condition, only 3 of them short. Doubling it doubles the evidence at no cost in session length.

3 · Logging

Record where wrong presses aimed

A missed press currently records no direction, which is why the response-bias question cannot be closed from the data. One extra column settles it.

4 · Protocol

Alternate the order properly

Eight of eleven sessions ran no-filter first. The one reversal reproduced both results; making it systematic removes the confound rather than arguing about it.

5 · Analysis

Test the mechanism

Score every marker for background clutter from the baked detections and test whether the benefit scales with it. Answerable from data already on disk, before any new session.

6 · Design

Close the felt-safety gap

Half the participants worried about missing things while missing nothing. Signal that the periphery is still being monitored — the literature's recommendation is a non-visual channel, since audio-tactile guidance matched visual guidance while improving situational awareness.

Then freeze

The supervisor's directive stands: one more iteration at most, then stop changing the stimulus and collect the maximum sample. A "bad" result with deep analysis is a valuable outcome; a good result on shifting stimuli is not a result at all.

Non-visual fallback precedent: Marquardt et al. — audio-tactile guidance matched visual guidance in accuracy while improving situational awareness; multi-modal cues are the documented mitigation for the dimmed-periphery notification problem.

Close · what this adds

Four things that did not exist before, and one number that does.

Technical

A working subtractive-guidance system on consumer hardware

Diminished Reality on a video-passthrough headset with no OS-level access to the passthrough layer — a hybrid composite of system passthrough and app-processed camera feed for which no prior art was found. Ten mode variants, detector-free by design, inside frame budget.

Methodological

A probe that the filter dims honestly

A click-target engineered to survive multiplicative dimming without popping out — achromatic, contrast-complete, ramped, static, modulating the underlying pixels. This is what makes a subtractive manipulation testable at all: without it the measurement rewards the manipulation for free.

Empirical

A spatial dose–response, not a verdict

The benefit of peripheral dimming is localised to the band where the dimming is strong and attention can still reach — replicated in two independently collected phases, absent in the three neighbouring bands, and largest for brief events.

Empirical

A measured-versus-felt cost gap

Objective peripheral detection did not drop at any angle; half the participants nevertheless reported worrying they would miss things. Naming that dissociation is a contribution in itself — it relocates the remaining problem from perception to communication.

Software-defined visual noise cancellation — on hardware anyone can buy — that buys focus without charging for awareness, in a band we can now point to on a ruler.
