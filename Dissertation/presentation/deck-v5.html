1
The idea · starting from something you already do

We delete distraction for space and for sound. Almost never for sight.

Space

The library carrel

Wooden walls on three sides. Nothing is added to help you read — the room is simply taken away until only the page is left.

Sound

Noise-cancelling headphones

They do not make the lecture louder. They make everything else quieter. A product millions of people buy whose whole job is removal.

Sight

Arjuna and the sparrow's eye

In the Mahābhārata, Droṇa asks his students what they can see before they shoot. Only Arjuna answers: "only the eye of the bird." The oldest description of perfect visual focus — and it is described entirely by what has disappeared.

Three thousand years of admiring that, and for vision we still build the opposite. Every visual attention aid on the market adds something to the scene — an arrow, a highlight, a label, a notification. This project builds the missing third column.

Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva — Droṇa's archery test. The sound analogy is more than rhetoric: researchers have proposed "visual noise cancellation" as a direct parallel, noting that a headset's camera → processing → display path is the same shape as a headphone's microphone → processing → speaker.
2
The thing itself · before any theory

You draw a window around your work. Everything outside it fades.

1 · You paint the window

Hold the controller trigger and sweep it across whatever you want to keep — a desk, a monitor, a workbench. Release, and it locks.

2 · The rest dims over three seconds

Not a switch. A slow close, so it feels like the room settling rather than the lights going out.

3 · The window stays with the room

Turn your head and it does not follow you. It stays on the desk, like a hole cut in a wall.

4 · It gets out of your way

Move your head quickly and the effect drops away within a fifth of a second. Look back at your work and it returns.

Eleven different versions of "fades" were built — dark, greyed, blurred, outlined, grainy, colour-filtered. Which one you should use turns out to be an empirical question with a surprising answer.
▶
Video — the whole interaction, once through
Screen recording from the headset: paint the window with the trigger, watch the three-second close, pan the head to show the window staying on the desk, then a fast turn away and back. Forty seconds, no narration needed. This should be the first thing the examiner sees, before any diagram.
The field's name for this is diminished reality — techniques that conceal, remove or see through parts of the real world in a headset view. Established as a research area by Mori, Ikeda & Saito's 2017 survey. Most prior work removes whole objects; this project dims whole regions instead, which is cruder but fast enough to run live.
3
Is the idea any good? · three things that have to be true

"Dimming the clutter should help" is a hunch. Here is what would make it a project.

Claim 1

The clutter actually costs you something

If having stuff in the corner of your eye does not measurably damage your performance, then there is nothing to recover and this is decoration. I need a published number, not a feeling.

Claim 2

Taking it away gives that something back

And crucially — taking it away how? Dark, grey, blurred, outlined? These act on completely different parts of the visual system. There is no reason to expect them to work equally well.

Claim 3

You can actually build it

On a headset people already own. If it needs a custom optical bench, it is a lab demonstration and not an interface.

So the first real work was not writing code. It was reading roughly 110 papers to find out whether anyone had answered these already — and what happened when they tried.

Claims 1 and 3 came back supported. Claim 2 came back with something more useful than support: hard evidence that several of the obvious ways of doing this actively fail. Those negative results shaped this system far more than the positive ones did.
Corpus of ~110 sources across the major human–computer interaction and mixed-reality venues (CHI, UIST, ISMAR, IEEE VR, DIS), plus vision science and human factors journals, 1998–2026.
4
Evidence for · and it names the right thing to measure

The cost is real, and it shows up in mistakes — not in slowness.

Claim 1, answered

Distractions in the corner of your eye, 66 people

Participants did a simple attention task in a virtual classroom. Adding movement and clutter at the edges of vision made them press when they should not have 2.4× as often (1.33 → 3.15 errors) and miss things they should have caught 8× as often (0.14 → 1.18). Both differences were overwhelmingly reliable.

And their speed did not change at all. That second half matters as much as the first: it says if you measure reaction time you will see nothing, and conclude wrongly that there was no cost. Mistakes are the currency. This fixed what our own study would measure, before we collected anything.

Claim 2, partly answered

Someone had already dimmed distractions and measured it

Two experiments with engineers and NASA staff doing a demanding assembly task while being distracted. Dimming everything irrelevant beat cleverly dimming only the specific offending object — fewer errors and lower reported effort. The blunt instrument won.

Why it should work at all

Irrelevant sights use up the same headspace as the task

Cognitive Load Theory's central point: processing you did not need competes for the same limited pool as processing you did. So dimming does not make you smarter or the task easier — it stops something else from spending your capacity.

Why dimming specifically

Attention chases contrast

The standard computational model of attention says your eye is pulled to wherever something stands out most sharply from its surroundings. Raise that local contrast and you attract the eye; flatten it and you repel the eye. A flattened periphery stops shouting.

And it is a dial, not a switch

How transparent the overlay is predicts where people look

Measured directly with eye tracking on a purpose-built dataset. So an overlay is an attention instrument with a controllable strength, not just a visual style.

Sources · Impact of Visual Distractors in VR on Sustained Attention, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience (2025), N = 66. McLaughlin et al. (2025), Human Factors. Cognitive Load Theory via Buchner et al. Itti, Koch & Niebur (1998). Duan et al. (2022), SARD dataset.
5
Evidence against · the results that pruned the design

Four published findings say the obvious ways of doing this fail.

Rules out blurring the surroundings

102 people, five techniques compared side by side

Blurring everything outside the focus area is the most natural way to "soften" a distraction. In a head-to-head comparison it came last: the most obvious of the five, and it actively annoyed 59 of the 102 participants. The authors' summary is blunt — no method stayed unnoticed, and this one was the worst offender.

Rules out greying things out

Darkening versus draining the colour, measured with eye tracking

Three ways of steering attention in 360° video were compared directly. Darkening an area worked. Draining the colour out of it did almost nothing. Greying out the surroundings — the other intuitive design — barely moves attention at all.

Rules out the obvious way to make it dynamic

Dimming harder as the head turns made people sick

The natural way to build a moving effect is to strengthen it as you turn. Tested against no effect at all, it increased motion sickness rather than reducing it. The obvious coupling has the wrong sign.

Rules out blacking everything out by default

Three studies agree that total removal is too much

Asked how they wanted reality diminished, people chose partial fading over complete removal, and asked for some hint that something was still there. Sparse "grains" of noise beat solid black on task performance, with 15 of 20 preferring the grains. And separately: anxiety was raised in every condition that narrowed the visual field, more so the narrower it got.

One more, from the study this project descends from most directly: an attempt to steer attention by subtly re-grading the real world did pull people's eyes to the target — but participants noticed it. The dream of an invisible manipulation is not available. So this project stopped chasing invisibility and designed for noticeable-but-tolerable instead.
Sources · Grogorick, Albuquerque & Magnor (2018), N = 102. Area-darkening vs desaturation eye-tracking comparison, IEEE (2021). Norouzi, Bruder & Welch (2018). Cheng et al., CHI 2022; Cao, Grandi & Kopper (2021); Barhorst-Cates, Rand & Creem-Regehr (2016). Sutton et al., UIST 2022.

The gap · why this had not been built

The two closest studies both had to fake the hardware.

2022

What people want from it

Established that users prefer partial fading and want context preserved. Run entirely inside a virtual-reality simulation of the effect, because no headset could actually do it. A study of an imagined system.

2025

That it improves performance

Established that dimming distractions reduces errors. Also inside a fully virtual environment, with the dimming done by the virtual scene itself rather than applied to a real room.

This work

The part both were missing

A working version on a real headset, dimming a real room. And a design that puts a real-hardware test and a simulated test in front of the same people — so if the two agree, it tells the field their simulations were trustworthy all along.

Demand is documented

People are asking for exactly this

A 2026 study co-designed with fifteen students with ADHD arrived at the same concept independently — user-configurable filters that blur, grey out, or remove parts of the environment. They built no prototype. A separate 2026 study on Apple's headset hid specific people to reduce social discomfort, but by covering them up rather than guiding attention.

The hardware appetite too

Others solved it with optics, expensively

One system defocuses regions of the real world using electrically tunable lenses. Another explores custom hardware for augmenting peripheral vision. Both accept serious optical complexity to get region-by-region control of what you see. Getting the same capability out of a shader on a €550 headset is the shift this dissertation documents.

The technical problem · and it is a wall, not a difficulty

You cannot dim the real world, because you are not allowed to look at it.

The display — what the user seesassembled by the operating system
Your app's layerfull control — but only in front
▲   the system stacks these together   ▲
The rebuilt view of the real roomcannot read it · cannot change it · cannot remove it

On a Quest 3 you never look at your room directly. Cameras capture it, the headset rebuilds it, and you look at a screen. That rebuilt image belongs to the operating system. Your app is allowed to draw on top of it and nothing else — the image is never handed to your code.

So the obvious way to build this — read the pixel, darken it, put it back — is not hard on this platform. There is no way to express it at all.

And this will not be relaxed

The restriction is deliberate

That camera view contains your home, your workplace and anyone standing near you. Letting apps read it is a privacy problem. Letting them rewrite it is a safety problem — an app could quietly alter the world you are walking through. Both concerns get stronger over time, not weaker.

So a project that assumes this opens up later is betting against the manufacturers' own interests. This one bets the opposite way and asks what can be built inside the restriction. That is what gives the answer a shelf life.

Nor is it a Meta quirk

Everyone else is stricter

On Apple's headset, camera pixels need a business licence that cannot be used in the consumer app store, and the only control over the room's appearance dims all of it at once. On see-through glasses the wall is physical rather than legal: they add light to what you see and have no way to subtract it. On those, "draw black" means "draw nothing."

Meta passthrough documentation; Apple visionOS enterprise camera-access documentation (2024); Itoh et al. (2021) on the optics required to make see-through glasses capable of blocking light.

What you are allowed to do · three levels, each with a hard ceiling

Three kinds of access. Only one of them can treat one part of your view differently from another.

LevelWhat it lets you doWhat that buysWhere it stops
1 · RecolourTurn brightness, contrast and colour saturation up or down on the rebuilt room view, or push it through a colour lookup tableYou can grey out or tint the whole room without touching a camera pixelUniformly, or not at all. No blur. No shaping it to a region. No leaving one area alone. And you still cannot read the image back.
2 · Draw overPut your own semi-transparent shapes in front of the room viewDimming, blacking out, covering up — and, importantly, leaving a hole where the untouched real view shows throughYou can only darken or hide. You cannot recolour, sharpen or blur what is behind, because your shape has no idea what is behind it.
3 · Use the camera yourselfTake the raw forward camera feed as an image your code can processAnything you like, pixel by pixel: blur, colour grading, glare reductionYou get one camera at 1280×960 covering about 87° — against the roughly 110° stereo view the system was already giving you. Plus extra delay, and it heats the headset.
The finding hiding in level 1

It is not that nothing is possible — it is that nothing local is possible

You can grey out your entire field of view with one line of code. But guiding attention means treating this bit differently from that bit — that is the whole definition. Level 1 cannot express "this bit" at any price. The missing capability is spatial, not chromatic, and saying so precisely is a contribution in itself.

Why write it down as three levels

It makes future hardware comparable

Three walls define the space: you cannot read the real view, you cannot shape an effect onto it, and on see-through glasses you cannot darken at all. Any future headset can be placed in this picture by asking which of the three walls it knocks down.

One historic exception existed at level 1 — projecting the room view onto your own surfaces, with per-surface recolouring — and it was withdrawn from the platform. Downgrading to an older version is not a route out: shipped apps must build against a supported version, the withdrawal was a deliberate platform decision rather than a bug, and an architecture resting on a removed feature could not be published or maintained. So the system is built only on behaviour the platform is committed to keeping.

Level 3's catch · the trap that makes the naive design worse than nothing

The camera you are given sees less than the view you would be replacing.

So the straightforward version backfires

Rebuild the whole view and you have downgraded everything

Take the camera feed, process it, and paint it over the user's whole field of view, and you have just given them: one camera instead of two, lower resolution, extra lag, and a view that is about 23° narrower on each side than the one you replaced.

And the strip you lose is the far edge of vision — which is precisely the region a peripheral-attention system exists to act on.

Plus a limit on what you can even test

These headsets are already below normal eyesight

Careful measurement shows no current camera-based headset matches normal human acuity at any light level, and the Quest 3 is noticeably worse in dim rooms. So any task needing fine real-world detail measures the headset, not your effect.

Which leaves the real question: how do you process the edges of someone's vision without degrading the middle, where they are actually working?

The answer · and it is the dissertation's main technical claim

The best possible way to show someone reality is to draw nothing at all.

Inside the window, the app draws nothing

Not "draws something transparent" — draws nothing. So what reaches the eye there is the headset's own rebuilt view of the room, at its full quality, its full 110°, and its own delay compensation. Better than anything the app could produce, and it costs nothing to achieve.

Everything is spent at the edges

All the processing happens where human eyesight is worst anyway. The same single decision fixes both problems from the last slide: the camera's narrow view no longer matters, because the camera is only ever used where the eye cannot resolve detail regardless.

Stated generally, this is the level-2 insight the dissertation contributes: when you are only allowed to draw over reality, the highest-quality way to render reality is to not render it. Leaving a hole is a documented technique — but every previous use puts virtual content around the hole. Using the hole as the focus region of a dimmed world appears to be new.

The shipped shape: a clear core 8° wide and 6° tall, fading out over the next 24°. Slide 21 shows why those specific numbers.

Verified in the shipped scene: window half-extents 8° × 6°, soft edge 24°, giving half strength at 20° and full strength at 32°. The prior-art search covered the major venues 2022–2026, platform documentation, and all 136 public forks of Meta's camera-access sample repository.

Making it hold still · two different meanings of "stays put"

The window has to belong to the room, not to your head.

Wrong · what you get for free

Stuck to your head

Define the window in screen coordinates and it follows your gaze everywhere, like a torch beam. It would chase your attention rather than anchor it — and tying a visual restriction to head movement is exactly the design that was shown to make people sick.

Better · the default here

Locked to a direction

The window is stored as a compass bearing and elevation, worked out fresh for every pixel from which way it faces in the world. Turn your head and the window stays over the desk. This handles all rotation correctly.

What it does not handle is walking. It is a fixed direction from wherever your eyes currently are, so if you step sideways the window slides across the room with you.

Best · anchored to the object

Locked to the thing itself

On releasing the trigger, the system fires four rays — one through each corner of the window you just painted — at the real geometry of the room, and remembers the four physical points where they land.

Every frame it re-derives the window from those four points and your current head position. Now you can walk toward the desk, around it, or away, and the window behaves like an actual hole cut in a wall — growing as you approach, shrinking as you retreat, shifting perspective as you move around it.

Engineering notes

What it took to make reliable

It needs no shader changes at all — the shader already consumes a bearing-and-elevation rectangle, so re-deriving that rectangle from world points each frame is enough to make it perspective-correct. It fails softly: if any corner ray misses — out of range, outside the depth sensor's view, or a reflective or transparent surface — the window quietly reverts to direction-locking. And because the depth system starts up asynchronously, an early attempt used to fail permanently; it now retries for up to ten seconds.

▶
Video — walking around an anchored window
The clearest demonstration in the project. Anchor the window to an object, then walk toward it, around it, and back. The window should grow, shift and shrink exactly as a real hole would. Shot on device — the depth rays do not function in the editor.
This is the object-anchored mode used for the real-hardware task in the study. The comfort argument against head-locking comes from Norouzi, Bruder & Welch (2018), who found head-coupled visual restriction increased sickness relative to none.
12
One camera, two eyes · a bug that was invisible in testing

Correct in each eye. Completely broken when you use both.

What happened

Double vision, on the device

The first version asked, for each pixel on screen, "what is the camera pixel at this same screen position?" That sounds right, and it is wrong: your two eyes see the same point in the room at different places on their screens, so each eye was handed a different camera pixel for the same object. Your visual system cannot merge that.

The fix

Ask "which direction is this?" instead

Each pixel already knows which direction in the room it is looking. Feed that direction through a simple model of where the camera was pointing, and you get the camera pixel for that direction. A direction is the same for both eyes, so both eyes get the same pixel, and the camera image behaves like paint on the walls of the room. It merges.

The general lesson, and one of the more transferable results here: when rendering for two eyes, whether your lookup depends on which eye is asking is a correctness question, not a performance question. The original code passed every single-eye test it was given.
The camera's viewing angles are taken from the values the hardware reports about itself rather than assumed — that alone removed a slight size mismatch between the processed edges and the untouched window. Where both forward cameras are available the shader picks one per pixel rather than blending them, since blending two viewpoints reintroduces exactly the ghosting this scheme exists to remove.

Where they came from

Contrast flattening and the highlight-plus-darkened-surround pairing come from a 2011 saliency study; the speckles from work showing grains beat blackout; the surviving outlines from the finding that people want a hint of what is still there; the warm/cool pair from early gaze-steering research.

All eleven share one shader and one geometry — a filter is just a rule for choosing a colour and an opacity for each pixel, given how far outside the window it sits. That is why eleven of them cost almost nothing to maintain, and why the design space could be explored properly instead of settling on the first thing that worked.

Narrowing down · what got dropped, and why

I built the most intuitive filter first. The evidence says don't ship it.

Dropped — and it was the original flagship

Blur

The most elaborate filter in the codebase and the first one built. It blurs in two rings of samples rather than one, which gives it a free estimate of how much detail it just destroyed — and it uses that to add fine grain back, and to restore local contrast. Both of those are published fixes for the known problems with peripheral blur: your eye detects blur mainly by noticing the loss of contrast, and putting the contrast back roughly doubles how much blur people tolerate.

Dropped anyway. In the 102-person comparison, blurring the surroundings was the most obtrusive of five techniques and annoyed more than half the participants. I implemented both published mitigations and still could not justify putting it in front of participants as the thing being tested.

Never promoted to a filter of its own

Greying things out

The other intuitive design, and the one people ask for by name. But when darkening and colour-draining were compared head to head with eye tracking, darkening steered attention and colour-draining did almost nothing. So greying appears here only as one ingredient inside a compound filter — never on its own.

Smart noise cancellation · the design idea the whole system is built around

Good noise cancelling doesn't mute everything. It lets the announcement through.

The design principle

Pass the signal, mute the noise

Headphones have moved past blanket silence. They listen, decide what matters — a voice, a platform announcement, a siren — and pass that through while everything else stays suppressed. Nobody calls that a compromise; it is what makes the thing usable in the world.

This system is the visual version of that, and it is the point of the whole architecture: dim the street, but let the traffic light through. Blanket dimming is the crude version; deciding what deserves to pass is the design.

Why a colour rule is not enough

It cannot tell important red from decorative red

Keeping reds, oranges and greens vivid faithfully preserves every brake light — and also every neon shopfront, every red jacket, every advertising hoarding. It keeps a colour, when what matters is a thing.

So the filter is given eyes

An object detector decides what passes

YOLO is a standard real-time object detector: one pass over an image returns boxes saying "traffic light here, stop sign there." It runs with a deliberately narrow remit — two categories, traffic lights and stop signs — and hands the boxes to the shader.

What the shader does with a box

Open a hole in the filter, then push the object forward

Three things happen at each detected object, and they matter for reading the results later:

1 · The filter is lifted off it. A window opens over the object at full strength regardless of where it is — inside or outside the focus region — because revealing the real object is the whole point.
2 · The object is boosted in colour and brightness, and breathes gently at about once a second.
3 · A ring just outside it is darkened, so it stands out from both directions at once rather than merely glowing.

And this only happens with the filter on. In the no-filter condition the detector's output is suppressed along with everything else — deliberately, so a baseline condition does not quietly highlight signs. That makes the comparison in this study "nothing" versus "dim and pass the signals through", not dimming alone. Slide 29 shows why that matters for what the results mean.
The honest counter-argument

Cleverer is not automatically better

The closest published study compared exactly these two strategies — dim everything, versus dim selectively using knowledge of what things are — and found dimming everything won. Fewer errors, less reported effort. So selectivity is not assumed superior here.

Live detection versus a perfect record

Why the study used a pre-computed track

Live, the detector runs on the headset. For the study it is replaced by a pre-computed record of every sign and light in the footage. This separates "is this interface any good?" from "is this detector any good?" — otherwise every result would partly be a measurement of the detector.

The selective filter · what got tested on the moving scene

SignPop: everything fades except the things that actually matter.

outside, ordinary < outside, signal < inside, ordinary < inside, signal

Four levels of prominence, on two tracks. Where a pixel lands depends on two things: whether it belongs to a detected signal, and how far outside the window it sits.

Ordinary things

Nearly untouched inside the window — just enough colour taken out that the window does not look artificially vivid against its own surroundings. Graded down to dim grey outside it.

Signals

Inside the window: colour boosted by 70%, mid-tone contrast pushed using a published curve, brightness lifted slightly. Outside: natural colour, merely dimmed — a deliberate decision, because in a monitoring task a traffic light at the edge of vision must never disappear entirely.

Headlight glare

Pixels that are bright, colourless and not part of a signal get compressed toward a visible dim spot — never to black, so the oncoming vehicle stays legible while its glare stops washing out the scene. One guard protects the case this would otherwise ruin: before dimming a blown-out white pixel, the shader checks whether it is surrounded by saturated colour. If it is, it is the middle of a lamp, and it is left alone.

A perceptual detail

Why this filter's edge is wider

A boundary between colour and grey looks harsher than a boundary between light and dark. So this filter widens its fade to 24° from the window edge, against the 18° the plain dark filters use. The more visible the change, the gentler the transition has to be.

And the window is small on purpose

The window is the dose

A windscreen-sized window leaves the whole usable view untouched and guides nothing. The clear core is deliberately narrow — 8° by 6° — with a long fade, so that most of what you look at is somewhere on the gradient rather than fully in or fully out.

The other thing worth muting · not an object, but a condition

Glare doesn't just hide what it covers. It hides everything else too.

The mechanism

A bright source lays a veil across your whole retina

Light from an oncoming headlight does not travel cleanly to one spot on the retina. It scatters inside the eye and deposits a wash of luminance over the entire image — so the contrast of everything you were trying to look at drops, not just the part near the lamp. This is called disability glare, and it is quantified by a standard formula relating the veil to the source's brightness and its angle from where you are looking.

It gets worse with age and with any clouding of the eye's optics, and it is a documented risk factor in night driving.

Why a camera headset makes it worse

The camera clips what the eye would merely find bright

A passthrough camera has far less dynamic range than the eye. A lamp that you would perceive as bright arrives at the display as a blown-out white blob with no detail in it at all — and the vehicle attached to it goes with it.

What the shader does

A soft knee, applied only where glare actually lives

Pixels that are bright, colourless, and not a detected signal — the signature of headlight glare rather than of a lamp that means something — are rolled off above a threshold rather than clipped. They are compressed toward a visible dim spot, never to black, so the oncoming vehicle stays legible while its veil is pulled down. Gentle in the focus window, strong in the periphery.

The guard that makes it safe

Never destroy the middle of a real lamp

A clipped white core is exactly what a bright traffic light looks like, so the glare rule would erase the one thing it must never touch. Before dimming any blown-out pixel the shader samples a small ring around it: if the surround is saturated colour — the lamp's coloured fringe — the pixel is exempt. Glare gets compressed; signals keep their core.

Where the technique comes from

Photographic tone reproduction

Rolling highlights off with a knee rather than clipping them is the standard move in high-dynamic-range imaging, going back to Reinhard's photographic tone reproduction and the filmic curves that followed — bright areas taper instead of slamming into white. The known weakness of those curves is that they are applied globally and are not content-aware. Gating the knee on a colour-and-brightness mask, with a guard for signal cores, is a direct response to that weakness.

And the hardware alternative

Others solve this with optics

There is a whole line of work on occlusion-capable see-through displays — spatial light modulators, per-pixel dimming layers, pixelated dimming filters — built specifically to stop bright ambient light washing out the view. That is the same goal, reached with added optical hardware. Here it is a few lines of shader on a headset that already exists.

What is not claimed. This ran in every filtered pass of the study — it is part of the same grading path — but nothing in the evaluation isolates or measures it. And the test footage is already dynamic-range-compressed compared to a real night scene, so the mask had far less to bite on than it would in life. Any glare benefit here is indicative only, and is reported as an engineering feature rather than a result.

The design rule

Run the sickness finding backwards

Strengthening the effect as the head turns made people sick. So this does the opposite: the effect drops away while you are moving and comes back when you settle. Exactly what noise cancellation does when you lift a cup — it yields to the thing you turned toward.

The moving filter yields most easily — as intended

SignPop is the filter used on a dynamic scene, where spatial awareness matters most, so it should be the readiest to step aside. It is: it gives way at half the head speed the blackout needs.

But the second column runs the wrong way

It also comes back the fastest

By the same reasoning it should stay away longest, giving the most awareness. It does not — at 0.7 seconds it returns soonest, and the blackout stays away longest at 1.5 seconds. The two columns are ordered inconsistently and the shipped values contradict the design intent. Worth correcting in the build; recorded here rather than quietly smoothed over.

Two more rules

Asymmetry, and never losing your work

Out in 0.20 s, back over 0.70 s: yielding has to feel instant to be trusted, returning can be leisurely because nothing is at stake. And yielding hides the effect rather than resetting it — glance away mid-formation and the three-second close keeps running behind the scenes, so you come back to where you were.

None of this was manipulated in the study. These settings were fixed and identical across every condition. They are design, presented as design — no claim attaches to them.
Values read from the shipped build. The three-second gradual close is motivated by work showing that a change introduced slowly enough stays below the threshold of notice while still influencing where people look. Per the literature review, these timing behaviours are among the least-studied parameters in this area — which is why every one is exposed as adjustable rather than fixed in code.

What is actually being claimed

This does not make you focus better. It makes distraction cost you less.

Not the claim

"A filter that makes you more attentive"

Nothing in the mechanism supports that. Dimming adds no capacity, sharpens no perception, trains no skill. If you were already doing well in a quiet room, this has nothing to offer you — and the data shows exactly that happening.

The claim

"A filter that stops something else spending your capacity"

Irrelevant sights consume the same limited headroom the task needs. Suppress them and you get that headroom back. So the benefit only exists when there is distraction to remove, and should grow with how much there is.

Two questions follow, and they are the two tests.

Question 1 · the benefit

Can you do less badly on a demanding task when there are distractions around you but you have the filter?

Note the phrasing — not "better than your best," but "less damaged than you would otherwise be." Tested on a real headset, with real distractions in the participant's own room.

Question 2 · the price

Does the selective version work without costing you awareness of your surroundings?

The dangerous version of a focus aid is one that works by making you blind to everything else. So the second test measures the benefit and the price in the same task at the same moment, and treats "it costs you nothing" as a claim that has to survive a test rather than a hope.

This is the wording the study was registered with before any data was collected, not a reinterpretation after the fact.

Test 2 · design · the awareness task, and why the angles are the angles

Markers appear at known angles — placed where the literature puts them.

How spare attention is measured
A standard method: a small light appears at a scheduled moment and you press as soon as you notice it. Slower or missed presses mean less attention left over. It is written into an international standard, and the research version that preceded it used a band from 11° to 23°.

What we used instead of a light

Ring markers placed on authored targets at known positions in a moving scene, so the moment each appeared, where it was, and whether it was caught are all recorded by the machine rather than judged from video afterwards.

The point of the ruler

Every boundary used in the analysis is a published landmark, not a line drawn after seeing the data. And the shipped filter lands on that ruler by construction: the clear core stops where drivers' gaze actually stops, the fade spans the standard probe band, and full strength arrives just past the edge of what you can take in at a glance.

A check done before the analysis

The markers are dimmed by the same filter as everything else.

Verified in the shader and the shipped scene

A marker fades exactly like a real object would

The markers are drawn through the filter, not on top of it. At the edge of vision, where the filter is at full strength, a marker is dimmed and drained of colour just as a real sign would be. So in the filtered condition the markers were harder to see, not easier.

Why this settles something important

The obvious objection runs backwards

The first thing anyone should ask about "dimming made peripheral things easier to notice" is whether the marker simply stood out more against a darkened background — a drawing artefact rather than an attention effect.

Because the marker is dimmed along with the scene, that explanation predicts the opposite of what was found. Any improvement has to be paid for out of attention.

And the markers are designed not to grab you

Deliberately unobtrusive

They fade in rather than snapping on, because sudden appearances capture attention automatically and would swamp the effect being measured. They do not move. And their pattern modulates the pixels underneath rather than covering them, so they keep their own internal contrast even when the filter multiplies everything down.

One thing this does not settle. The marker's own fading is computed from the window falloff alone, so a detection window opening nearby does not un-dim the marker — that part is clean. But it does un-dim the object underneath. So the fair statement is narrow: the improvement cannot be explained by the marker gaining contrast, because it did not. What it can be explained by is the smart filter letting the object through, which is the subject of slide 29.

Where this method is stronger

Nobody else did it on real hardware

Every comparison study either simulated the effect in VR or used a bench rig that cannot dim anything. This one dims an actual room on a headset you can buy — and runs a real-hardware task and a simulated task past the same people, which is what lets it say anything about whether their simulations were trustworthy. (Slide 7.)

Where it is weaker — and it matters

No eye tracker, so no gaze data

Sutton and Grogorick both measured where people looked, directly. This headset has no eye tracker, so attention is inferred from what people detected and how fast. The honest limit: this study can say a marker was caught 18.6 points more often, but not that anyone's gaze moved. Every claim here is a behaviour claim, never a gaze claim.

Why headcount is the wrong question
These designs get their sensitivity from showing the same person both conditions and repeating the comparison many times within each session — not from recruiting more people. Each participant is their own control, which removes the largest source of noise: that people differ enormously from one another. Twelve people compared against themselves beats fifty compared against each other.
Two tests behind every number
Each result on the next slides carries two p-values — a standard paired test, and a second one that assumes nothing about the shape of the data. Reporting both is deliberate: the transform that this field used for years to handle awkward data has recently been shown to have unreliable error rates, so the safer move is to run the assumption-free test alongside and only believe results that survive both.
The measure is not improvised. Timing a button press to a scheduled stimulus is a standardised method for indexing spare attention — an ISO standard for in-vehicle testing, with roots in driving research from 2005. The 100–2500 ms scoring window used here is taken from it rather than chosen to suit the data.

One exclusion, kept but set aside

One participant, on the moving-scene task only

Their filtered run recorded 539 presses that hit nothing — continuous trigger-pulling rather than responding to markers. The worry is obvious: enough random presses will hit some markers by luck and inflate the score.

So I tested it rather than assuming. Every scored catch records how far off-centre the press was. The safety question, answered

A loss bigger than about 5 points can be ruled out

Before collecting anything, the study fixed a line in the sand: losing more than 10 points of peripheral awareness would be unacceptable in a monitoring context, whatever the focus benefit.

The data comes in on the right side of that line. The measured change is positive, and the plausible range runs from −4.9 to +12.6 points. The worst case the data supports is a loss of about five points — comfortably inside the limit. The filter did not blind people to their surroundings.

Being precise about what has and has not been shown: the formal test asks the range to sit inside both limits, and it does not — it extends past +10 at the top. But that end is the improvement side, which is not a hazard. On the side that matters for safety, the answer is clean.
Why a limit was set in advance
"We found no difference" is a weak statement — you can always fail to find a difference by not looking hard enough. Naming the smallest loss you would care about, before collecting data, converts it into a claim that could have failed. It did not fail.
And the honest limit

The overall number hides opposite effects

Averaged over the whole field of view, +3.9 points is not itself a reliable difference. But that average pools regions the filter barely touches with regions it acts on completely — the strength varies from nothing to full across the visual field.

So the pooled number answers the safety question and nothing else. Where the filter actually did something is the next slide — and the pattern there is exactly what the filter's own shape predicts.

Results · Test 2, question 2 continued · where it helped and where it did not

Better exactly where the filter acts and the eye still works. Worse outside both.

The shape is the finding

Two conditions, and both are needed

Remember what the filter does at a detected signal: it opens a hole in itself over that object, by the same amount everywhere. So what changes with angle is not whether the hole opens — it is how much difference opening it makes, which is precisely the local filter strength.

Below 10° the filter is effectively off, so lifting it reveals nothing that was not already visible. Beyond 30° lifting it reveals the most of all — but the target sits outside the region the eye can use without moving, so the reveal is not picked up. Only at 20–30° are both conditions met: a strong filter to lift, and an eye that can still use the result.

That is the pattern the filter's own shape predicts, worked out from the shader rather than fitted to the data afterwards.

"Percentage points"
The plain difference between two percentages. Going from 40% to 58% is a rise of 18 points — not 18%, which would be a much smaller change.
Only the 20–30° ring is individually reliable (p = 0.028). The other three are small and within noise, which is the correct reading of a flat bar — not evidence of harm.

A method point that changed the answer

The markers move, so "which band was it in?" had to be rebuilt

Markers travel a long way while visible — typically 17°, and 45 of the 82 cross a band boundary during their lifetime. Scoring by their average position would have blurred the exact distinction this analysis depends on: 32% of catches happened in a different band from the marker's average position. So each marker's full path was reconstructed from the recorded scene track and every press scored against where the marker actually was at that instant. The reconstruction reproduces the authored positions exactly for 75 of the 82 markers, and was checked independently against a second recorded measurement.

This is a secondary analysis, on eleven people, with a chance of detection of about 64%. The agreement across the two phases is the strongest support available at this size — and it is support, not a substitute for a larger confirmatory study.

Results · what people said · ten questionnaires

Section A — did it help you concentrate

47 out of 50

The three core items — whether the filter helped them concentrate, whether the window was in the right place, whether they could work in it — were answered positively by all ten people.

Section B — awareness and comfort

29 out of 50

This section asked whether they still felt aware of what was around them, whether they would have noticed something happening outside the window, and whether anything felt uncomfortable or disorienting.

Roughly half reported feeling cut off from their surroundings.

And that directly contradicts the measurement. The behavioural result on slide 25 rules out a loss of more than about five points of peripheral awareness — people were catching things at the edge of vision just as well, and in the targeted ring rather better. Objectively their awareness held up. Subjectively, half of them felt it had not.
Explanation 1

The feeling is of the periphery being changed, not of missing things

The evidence says no manipulation of this kind stays unnoticed. People can plainly see that their surroundings have been altered — and "I can tell something has been done to my vision" is a very different experience from "I failed to notice something," even when only the first is true.

Explanation 2

Narrowing the field raises anxiety even when performance is fine

Measured directly: anxiety was elevated in every restricted-field condition tested, rising as the field narrowed — while spatial learning survived down to a very narrow field. Discomfort and capability came apart in that study exactly as they do here.

Why not just fix it

Reassuring people costs the thing that works

The obvious remedy — make the periphery more visible, or add a cue when something happens out there — reintroduces exactly the salience the filter exists to remove. That is the trade-off in its sharpest form, and it is unresolved. A promising route avoids vision entirely: guidance through sound or touch has been shown to match visual guidance for accuracy while improving awareness, and it would leave the visual channel untouched.

What the result actually shows · and what it cannot separate

It was not the dimming. It was dimming plus letting the signal through.

Nearly every marker in this study sits on a detected traffic light or sign — 70 of the 80 targets (88%) fall inside an active detection window while they are on screen. So in the filter condition, the object under the marker was having the filter lifted off it and being pushed forward, and in the no-filter condition it was not.

Which makes the mechanism concrete rather than speculative

The pass-through is the effect

The marker itself stays dimmed — its fading is computed straight from the window falloff and is not affected by the detection window. But the object beneath it pops. People were not spotting a brighter marker; they were spotting a traffic light that the smart filter had deliberately let through.

That is the design working as intended, and it explains the band pattern without appealing to anything unobserved.

The price of that clarity

The manipulation is a bundle, and this design cannot take it apart

Filter-on means all of it at once: dimming, detection, the hole opening on the object, the colour and brightness boost, the darkened surround, the gentle pulse. Nothing here says how much of the +18.6 came from each.

It also means the study cannot speak to the published finding that blunt universal dimming beat selective, knowledge-driven dimming — because only the selective version was ever run.

The next experiment writes itself

Three arms instead of two

No filter · filter with the detector off · filter with the detector on. The middle arm is the one this study is missing, and it is the arm that separates "dimming helped" from "passing the signal through helped."

It needs no new code — the detector already has a switch — and it would directly test the universal-versus-selective question on real hardware, which nobody has yet done.

One perceptual footnote worth keeping: even at equal treatment, how well you detect something at the edge of vision depends on what your attention is doing, and visual clutter actively masks low-contrast things near it. Both would add to the pass-through account rather than replace it — and both predict the benefit should grow with how cluttered the scene is at the marker's location, which is computable from the detection record already on disk.

Where this leaves things

What was built, what was found, and what I would not yet claim.

Built

A working subtractive interface, on a headset you can buy

Eleven filters in one shader. A way to get full-quality reality in the middle of your view and processed reality at the edges, on a platform that forbids touching reality at all. A window that anchors to a physical object. A camera image that merges properly across two eyes.

The two closest published studies had to simulate all of this inside VR. This is the part they were missing.

Found

A benefit, no measurable price, and one puzzle

Focus: 3.65 points more accurate on a demanding task with distractions present, in nine of eleven people.

Awareness: not lost. A loss greater than about five points can be ruled out.

The puzzle: in the ring the filter is aimed at, peripheral detection went up 18 points — with a testable explanation and the data already in hand to test it.

Not claimed

The honest boundary

The manipulation is a bundle. Dimming, detection, pass-through, boost, surround and pulse all switch together, so no number here attributes the gain to any one of them.

Eleven people, not the twenty planned — enough to detect these effects about six times in ten, so a null would have meant little.

The angle-band result is a secondary analysis, supported by agreeing across two independent phases rather than by a confirmatory test.

Half the participants felt cut off, and nothing here measures that feeling — only behaviour.

The glare compression ran but was never isolated, on footage that under-exercises it.

And no claims are made about driving, drivers or road safety; the moving footage is a stimulus, nothing more.

The one-sentence version: a filter that dims the surroundings but lets the important things through bought a real gain in focus, cost no measurable awareness — and in the ring where it actually acts, it made the edge of vision better rather than worse.

Next, in order: run the three-arm study — no filter, filter with the detector off, filter with it on — which separates dimming from pass-through and needs no new code; test the clutter prediction against the detection record already on disk; correct the inconsistent yield-and-return timings; and add the distraction-free condition this pilot did not include.
